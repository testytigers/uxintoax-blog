## The Short Answer

A UX portfolio isn't a gallery of pretty screens. It's a collection of case studies that tell a story: **here's a problem, here's how I solved it, and here's the impact.**

Recruiting managers spend an average of **2-3 minutes** scanning a portfolio. Your goal isn't to show everything you've ever done. It's to show that you think like a UX designer.

Here's how to build one that gets you hired.

---

## What a UX Portfolio Actually Needs

Forget the perfectionism. A hiring-quality portfolio needs exactly three things:

### 1. A Strong Introduction
Your name, what you do, and one sentence that captures your design philosophy.

**Examples:**
- "I design products that make complex data simple and accessible."
- "UX designer focused on creating inclusive experiences for underserved audiences."
- "I turn ambiguous problems into clear, user-centered solutions."

No fluff. No "passionate ninja rockstar" energy. Just clarity.

### 2. Three to Five Case Studies
Each one should follow a consistent structure (more on that below).

### 3. An About Page
Your background, your perspective, what you're looking for. Make it human.

That's it. Three things. Everything else is decoration.

---

## What Hiring Managers Actually Look For

Here's what separates a portfolio that gets interviews from one that gets ignored:

### They Want to See Your Thinking, Not Just Your Screens

The mistake most portfolios make: they show final designs without showing how they got there.

**Bad case study:** "Here's what I designed. Isn't it beautiful?"

**Good case study:** "Here's the problem, here's what I learned from users, here's what I tried, here's what worked, here's what I'd do differently."

### The Red Flags That Get Portfolios Rejected

- **No process** — Pretty screens with no research, no wireframes, no rationale
- **No constraints** — "I designed an entire app from scratch" with no real-world limitations
- **No metrics** — No way to measure whether the design actually helped
- **No reflection** — No discussion of trade-offs, failures, or what was learned
- **No storytelling** — Just a gallery with no narrative thread

## The Case Study Structure That Works

Each case study should follow this structure. It's proven. It works.

### 1. The Hook (2-3 paragraphs)

Start with the problem and why it matters.

**Template:**
> "[Company] was struggling with [problem]. [Number]% of users [negative outcome]. The challenge was to [goal] while [constraint]."

### 2. Your Role (1 paragraph)

Be specific about what you did.

**Template:**
> "As the sole UX designer on this project, I was responsible for [list 3-4 key responsibilities]. I worked with [stakeholders] over [timeline]."

### 3. The Research (2-3 sections)

This is where most portfolios fail. Show your work.

- **User interviews** — Quotes, findings, pain points
- **Competitive analysis** — What others do well, where they fall short
- **Data insights** — Analytics, surveys, behavioral observations
- **Synthesis** — What patterns emerged? What surprised you?

### 4. The Process (3-4 sections)

Show how you moved from research to design:

- **Problem framing** — How you defined the challenge
- **Ideation** — Sketches, wireframes, decisions you debated
- **Prototyping** — What you built and why
- **Testing** — What you learned from users

**Include screenshots of your sketches, whiteboard sessions, and rough drafts.** This proves you actually did the work.

### 5. The Outcome (2-3 sections)

What happened? What did you learn?

- **Design decisions** — Why you chose what you did
- **Results** — Metrics, user feedback, business impact
- **What I'd do differently** — Shows maturity and self-awareness

## Choosing Projects That Get You Hired

Not all projects are equal. Here's what to include and what to skip.

### Include Projects That Show...

**Diversity of skills:**
- One research-heavy project
- One interaction design project
- One visual design project
- One project with measurable impact

**Diversity of formats:**
- Mobile app
- Web application
- Design system or component library
- Service design or physical-digital hybrid

**Diversity of users:**
- B2B (business)
- B2C (consumer)
- Accessibility-focused
- International or multilingual

### Skip Projects That...

- Are purely visual with no research component
- Are theoretical exercises with no user validation
- Don't show your decision-making process
- Are too similar to each other

### The Spec Project Question

**Should I do a speculative redesign?**

Yes, but only if:
1. You do actual user research (not just your opinion)
2. You validate your designs with real users
3. You frame it as "exploring improvements to [X]" not "[X] is terrible, here's my fix"

Redesigns of existing products carry baggage — users already have habits and expectations. Approach with respect for the existing experience.

## Making Your Portfolio Itself a UX Case Study

Your portfolio website is your first UX project. If it's confusing, slow, or broken, it undermines every case study.

### Portfolio Must-Haves

- **Fast loading** — Under 3 seconds. Every extra second hurts your credibility
- **Clear navigation** — Home, Case Studies, About, Contact. Nothing more
- **Readable typography** — No decorative fonts. Readability over aesthetics
- **Mobile responsive** — Many hiring managers will view on their phone
- **Consistent design system** — Your portfolio should demonstrate strong visual design skills

### What to Avoid

- **Splash pages** — Nobody wants to click through an intro animation
- **Full-screen carousels** — Users skip them. Don't waste the top fold
- **Excessive animations** — They slow down your site and distract from content
- **Hidden contact info** — Make it easy to reach you
- **Stock photos** — They make your portfolio feel generic

## Writing Case Studies That Stand Out

The writing is as important as the designs. Here's how to make your case studies compelling:

### Use the STAR Method

- **Situation** — What was the context?
- **Task** — What were you asked to solve?
- **Action** — What did you do and why?
- **Result** — What happened and what did you learn?

### Write for Skimmers

Hiring managers don't read every word. They scan.

**Do this:**
- Use clear headings and subheadings
- Bold key insights
- Use bullet points for lists
- Keep paragraphs to 3-4 lines
- Put the most important info first

**Don't do this:**
- Walls of text
- Jargon without explanation
- Overly academic writing
- Starting with your education instead of the problem

### Show, Don't Just Tell

Every section of text should be accompanied by relevant visuals:

- Research → Screenshots of interview notes, personas, journey maps
- Process → Wireframes, sketches, flow diagrams
- Design → High-fidelity screens, prototypes
- Results → Metrics, user quotes, before/after comparisons

### Be Honest About Failures

Nothing shows maturity like admitting what didn't work.

**Example:**
> "Our first prototype failed with 4 out of 5 users. The navigation structure confused them. We went back, reorganized the information architecture, and retested. The second version had 80% task completion."

This shows:
- You test early
- You iterate based on feedback
- You're not afraid to kill your darlings

## Portfolio Platforms: Where to Build It

You have options. Each has pros and cons.

### Website Builders (Recommended)

**Webflow**
- Pros: Design freedom, no code needed, fast hosting
- Cons: Learning curve, paid ($14/month)

**Framer**
- Pros: Fast, beautiful templates, great for designers
- Cons: Less flexible than Webflow for complex sites

**Framer/Figma**
- Pros: Great if you're already in Figma, fast to publish
- Cons: Limited customization

### Developer Platforms

**GitHub Pages + Hugo**
- Pros: Free, great for showing technical skills
- Cons: Requires HTML/CSS knowledge

**Next.js / Astro**
- Pros: Full control, impressive technical skills
- Cons: Time investment, hosting costs

### Template-Based

**Notion**
- Pros: Fast to set up, easy to update
- Cons: Looks generic, limited design control

**Wix / Squarespace**
- Pros: Easy, all-in-one
- Cons: Template limitations, not UX-first

### My Recommendation

**Start with Webflow or Framer.** They give you the most design freedom without requiring code. Your portfolio is your brand — don't compromise on the experience.

## Getting Your Portfolio Reviewed

Before you publish, get feedback from people who matter:

### Who to Ask

- **Other UX designers** — They'll critique your process
- **Hiring managers** — They'll tell you what they're looking for
- **Recruiters** — They'll tell you how quickly they can find what they need
- **People outside UX** — They'll tell you if your writing is clear

### Where to Get Feedback

- **ADPList** — Free mentorship and portfolio reviews from experienced designers
- **Twitter/X** — Post snippets and ask for feedback
- **LinkedIn** — Share case studies and ask for comments
- **Local UX meetups** — In-person reviews are gold

### The 2-Minute Test

Ask someone to look at your portfolio for 2 minutes and then answer:
1. What do I do?
2. What kind of projects have I worked on?
3. What's my design approach?
4. How would I feel about hiring me?

If they can't answer these quickly, your portfolio needs work.

## Common Portfolio Mistakes (And How to Fix Them)

### ❌ "I'm not a real designer yet"
**Fix:** You don't need to be. Your portfolio shows what you can do. Every expert was once a beginner.

### ❌ "My portfolio isn't perfect"
**Fix:** It will never be. Ship it. You'll iterate. A published portfolio with room to grow beats a "perfect" one that exists only in your head.

### ❌ "I need a logo for my portfolio"
**Fix:** No. Your name is your brand. Focus on your work, not your logo.

### ❌ "I should include everything I've ever made"
**Fix:** Curate ruthlessly. Three strong case studies > ten mediocre ones.

### ❌ "I need a video intro"
**Fix:** Nobody watches it. Put your introduction in text. Let hiring managers scroll at their own pace.

## The Portfolio That Gets You Hired: A Checklist

Before you share your portfolio, verify:

- [ ] Homepage is clear and takes under 5 seconds to understand
- [ ] 3-5 case studies with clear structure
- [ ] Each case study shows research, process, and outcome
- [ ] Real user quotes and data included
- [ ] Before/after comparisons where possible
- [ ] About page with your story and perspective
- [ ] Contact information is easy to find
- [ ] Loads in under 3 seconds
- [ ] Mobile-responsive
- [ ] No typos or broken links
- [ ] Tested with 3+ people who gave honest feedback

## Final Thought

Your portfolio isn't a museum of your best work. It's a conversation starter. It's proof that you can think through problems, understand people, and create solutions that work.

The best portfolios aren't the prettiest. They're the ones that make a hiring manager lean back, close the tab, and think: "I need to talk to this person."

That's the goal. Build toward that.
